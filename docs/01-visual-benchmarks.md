# Visual benchmarks: what the best explainers do, and what we adopt

The brief was to look at how asset managers and others explain products visually, keep the best of it, and adapt it for a Ram Investments diary. This note lists what we looked at, what each does well, and where that idea shows up in the diary pages.

## The short version

The strongest product explainers share four habits:

1. **One fixed template, repeated.** Readers learn the layout once, then compare products by looking at the same spot on each page. Regulators impose this on purpose (EU key information documents, SEBI factsheets, IRDAI's customer information sheet).
2. **The regulator's own risk scale, not a home-made score.** Investors already see the SEBI riskometer and credit ratings. Reusing them is clearer and safer than a new "risk out of 5".
3. **One picture of the mechanism per product,** usually with a named person and real numbers, instead of decorative imagery.
4. **Saying who a product is not for.** Disclosure regimes in the EU and Australia require it, and it builds trust.

We combined these into a **field guide**: each product is an "entry" with the same two pages. The left page helps you recognise it; the right page helps you decide whether it fits. The diary format adds one thing brochures cannot: space to write your own numbers next to the explanation.

## What we chose not to do

- **"Return potential" stars or scores.** They imply returns, invite compliance problems and are subjective. Risk is shown only on scales the regulators define.
- **Radar or spider charts.** Hard to read at A5 and easy to misread.
- **Past performance, premium quotes and industry size figures.** They date before the diary is opened, and performance in a distributor's material brings extra advertising rules.
- **Stock photography.** It adds no information. Every graphic explains a mechanism.
- **Scheme, fund or insurer names.** The pages explain categories, which keeps them in investor-education territory and stops them from going stale.
